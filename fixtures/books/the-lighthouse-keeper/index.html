The Lighthouse Keeper's Ledger

a novella in six chapters

Chapter One — The Handover

Mara arrived at Stonewick Point on the last ferry of the season, carrying one suitcase and a box of logbooks that were not yet hers. The outgoing keeper, a man named Elric who had tended the light for thirty-one years, met her on the jetty with his coat already buttoned for leaving.

“There's a rhythm to it,” he said, handing her the key before she had even set down her case. “Four seconds on, four seconds off. You'll hear it in your sleep by November, and you'll miss it by March, when they finally send the automatic ones out here.”

She did not tell him that she had asked for this posting specifically because she wanted something with a rhythm to it. Her last three years had had none.

The lighthouse stood at the end of a spit of black rock, connected to the mainland by a causeway that flooded twice a day. Elric walked her through the generator room, the oil store, the narrow iron stairs that spiraled up through eleven floors of cold stone, and the lens room at the top, where the great glass drum turned slowly on its bath of mercury, catching the last grey light of the afternoon and throwing it out to sea in patient, unhurried sweeps.

“The sea doesn't care if you're paying attention,” Elric said, “but the ships do. That's the whole job, really. Paying attention when nobody's watching you do it.”

He left on the seven o'clock crossing, before the tide came in. Mara watched the ferry become a shape and then a light and then nothing, and turned to look at the tower behind her, and understood for the first time that she now lived inside a clock.

Chapter Two — The Logbook

The ledgers went back further than Elric's thirty-one years — back, in fact, to 1889, when the light was first lit. Mara spent her first storm-bound week reading them cover to cover by lamplight, tracing the handwriting of keepers she would never meet through decades of weather, supply shortages, and the occasional shipwreck logged in a hand gone suddenly unsteady.

Most entries were mundane by design. Wind direction. Barrels of oil remaining. A note about a jammed mechanism, repaired by evening. The job, as far as the ledger recorded it, was the diligent absence of incident.

But threaded through the routine were other things — a keeper in 1927 who recorded, without comment, that he had begun leaving a second lamp lit in the window facing the mainland, “for E., who is watching for it.” An entry from the winter of 1953 that simply read: Nothing to report, and I am grateful for it. A pressed and long-faded flower taped to the inside back cover of a ledger from 1971, with no explanation offered anywhere in the surrounding pages.

Mara began, without quite deciding to, keeping two records: the official one, terse and weather-bound as tradition demanded, and a private one, where she wrote what the official ledger had no room for.

Chapter Three — The Boat That Didn't Come In

In her second month, a fishing boat called the Kestrel Anne went out past the point in weather that everyone on the mainland had told its captain to wait out. Mara watched it go from the lens room, a smear of white against the slate-colored water, and felt the particular helplessness of someone whose only tool is light.

She kept the beam turning through the night, and radioed the coast guard station at the first sign the swell was worsening, and stood at the window until well past midnight watching for a shape that did not come.

The Kestrel Anne limped back at dawn under its own power, engine flooded, one crewman with a broken wrist, all four aboard alive because they had turned back the moment they saw the light holding steady through the spray — a fixed point, the captain said later, that told them which way was home when the compass had started arguing with itself.

Mara wrote it in the official ledger in four lines, as the form demanded. In her own book she wrote three pages, none of which she ever showed to anyone, about what it meant to be useful in exactly the shape the world needed and no other.

Chapter Four — Visitors

Word travels along a coast the way light does — bent by obstacles, but travelling anyway. By spring, Mara had acquired an irregular society of visitors: the harbourmaster's daughter, who came to sketch the tower from the rocks; a retired keeper named Owusu who arrived once a month on the low tide with a thermos and no apparent purpose beyond conversation; a young man from the university on the mainland who was writing something about optics and mercury baths and stayed for entire afternoons asking questions Mara only half-understood.

She had come to Stonewick Point wanting solitude, and had found instead a strange, loose-knit community organized entirely around a light that turned whether or not anyone came to see it.

“You'll notice,” Owusu told her one grey afternoon, stirring his tea with a driftwood twig because he never remembered to bring a spoon, “that everyone who visits a lighthouse eventually starts talking about their own life as if it needed one. Don't let it go to your head. It's the shape of the building. People can't help it.”

Chapter Five — The Automatic Light

The letter came in September, on official paper, informing her that Stonewick Point would be automated by the following spring, in line with the modernization schedule that had already claimed eleven other lights along the coast. A junior engineer would visit twice a year for maintenance. The keeper's cottage would be sold or, more likely, left to the weather.

Mara read the letter twice, then went up the eleven flights to the lens room and sat with her back against the cold iron rail, watching the drum turn in its silent, mercury-smooth arc, four seconds on, four seconds off, the rhythm Elric had promised her and that had indeed, as he'd said, become something she heard in her sleep.

She thought about the keeper of 1953 who was grateful to have nothing to report. She thought about the pressed flower with no explanation. She understood, for the first time with any clarity, that she was not the last of a tradition so much as one more entry in a ledger that would keep being written whether or not anyone was there to hold the pen — that the light did not need her, particularly, but that she had needed it, particularly, and that this was allowed to be true at the same time.

Chapter Six — The Last Entry

On her final evening at the light, Mara climbed to the lens room at dusk, as she had done nearly every evening for four years, and lit the lamp by hand one last time, though the automatic mechanism had already been installed and tested and would take over at midnight without her.

She wrote her last entry in the official ledger in the customary four lines. Then she opened her own book, the private one, and after a long while of watching the light sweep out over water she could no longer see the end of, she wrote a single line and closed the cover.

Four seconds on, four seconds off — and someone, somewhere, is always watching for it, whether or not they know your name.

She left the ledger, both of them, on the shelf where Elric had left his, for whoever might come after — engineer or ghost or simply the tide — to find, or not find, as the years saw fit.
